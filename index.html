Five Questions to Separate

Retirement isn't one decision — it's five overlapping ones. Knowing which question you're asking prevents costly mix-ups.

1

When to stop working

No single U.S. retirement age. Work, health, savings, and employer benefits drive this personal date.

2

When to claim benefits

Social Security can start at 62, FRA is 66–67, and delaying can increase benefits up to age 70.

3

How to cover healthcare

Medicare typically begins at 65, but enrollment timing and employer coverage change what makes sense.

4

How to draw income

401(k), IRA, pension, Social Security, and part-time work each have different tax and flexibility tradeoffs.

5

How to protect the family

Spousal benefits, survivor benefits, beneficiary forms, powers of attorney, and fraud prevention matter as much as balances.

Healthcare at 65+

Medicare Coverage Choices

Medicare is the main federal health program for people 65+. The two main tracks have very different cost structures, provider access, and flexibility.

FeatureOriginal Medicare (Part A + B + D + Medigap)Medicare Advantage (Part C)
Provider AccessAny doctor who accepts MedicareUsually limited to plan network
Out-of-Pocket CapNo built-in cap without MedigapAnnual limit on covered services
Drug CoverageSeparate Part D plan requiredUsually bundled in plan
Dental / Vision / HearingNot covered by Original MedicareSome plans include extras
Switching LaterFlexible; Medigap may need underwritingPlans change annually; Medigap return may need underwriting
Travel CoverageWorks nationwideMay be limited to plan area
Premium StructurePart B (~$175/mo) + Part D + MedigapOften low/$0 premium, but cost-sharing varies
⚠️ Coverage gap: Original Medicare does not cover most long-term care, routine dental, routine vision, or hearing aids. Plan for those costs separately. Medicare.gov source →

Retirement Account Types

Each account has different tax treatment, contribution limits, and withdrawal rules. Understanding the differences is essential for a smart drawdown strategy.

🏦

401(k) / 403(b)

Employer-sponsored. Pre-tax contributions reduce taxable income now. Taxed on withdrawal. Employer match is essentially free money — capture it first.

2025 limit: $23,500 (+$7,500 if 50+)
📘

Traditional IRA

May offer tax-deductible contributions depending on income. Good complement to a 401(k). Required Minimum Distributions begin at 73.

2025 limit: $7,000 (+$1,000 if 50+)
🌱

Roth IRA

After-tax contributions — qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free. No RMDs during your lifetime. Best if you expect higher taxes in retirement.

Tax-free growth & withdrawals
🏥

HSA

Triple tax advantage: deductible contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free medical withdrawals. After 65, can withdraw for any purpose (taxed like IRA).

Triple tax advantage
📈

Brokerage Account

No contribution limits. No tax advantages, but long-term gains taxed at lower capital gains rates. Excellent for bridging retirement income before 59½.

Flexible, no limits
📜

Annuities

Insurance products providing guaranteed income stream. Useful for covering fixed expenses but fees and terms vary widely — read every contract detail carefully.

Guaranteed income — read fine print
10-Year Plan

Your Roadmap to Retirement

Four focused phases for the experienced professional building toward their next chapter — from financial foundation to full freedom.

Phase 1
Ages 50–52 · Years 1–2

Foundation & Discovery

  • List every retirement account: balance, type, beneficiary
  • Maximize 401(k) including catch-up ($31,000/yr total)
  • Open Roth IRA if income-eligible; start conversion strategy if not
  • Run SSA.gov benefit estimate — check both spouses' records
  • Build 12-month emergency fund separate from retirement
Phase 2
Ages 53–55 · Years 3–5

Accelerate & De-Risk

  • Shift portfolio allocation gradually toward bonds
  • Pay down high-interest debt; set mortgage payoff timeline
  • Explore part-time or consulting income streams
  • Evaluate long-term care insurance while rates are lower
  • Draft or update will, power of attorney, and healthcare proxy
Phase 3
Ages 56–58 · Years 6–8

Transition & Position

  • Run Social Security breakeven analysis — decide when each spouse claims
  • Model healthcare bridge plan from retirement to Medicare at 65
  • Practice living on your retirement budget for 6 months
  • Consider Roth conversions in lower-income years to reduce future RMDs
  • Meet with a fee-only financial planner for a full income plan
Phase 4
Ages 59–60 · Years 9–10

Launch & Freedom

  • At 59½: penalty-free access to all accounts — full financial inventory
  • Finalize retirement date; negotiate a graceful exit with employer
  • Set up income streams: SS, portfolio withdrawals, consulting
  • Activate ACA marketplace, COBRA, or spouse's plan as bridge
  • Write your Retirement Mission Statement — purpose matters
